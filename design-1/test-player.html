Reading Passage 1

The history of education is, in many ways, the history of civilisation itself. Long before schools existed, knowledge was passed from one generation to the next through stories, songs and direct instruction within the family. Elders taught the young how to farm, hunt, build and live within their community, and this informal education was enough for societies that changed only slowly over hundreds of years.

The second stage in this long story began when writing was invented. Once knowledge could be recorded on clay, papyrus and later paper, it no longer depended on memory alone. The first formal schools appeared in ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, China and India, where small groups of students gathered around a teacher to learn reading, writing and mathematics. Education was still a privilege of the few, reserved mainly for the sons of rulers, priests and wealthy merchants, but the basic pattern of the classroom had been established.

The modern period transformed education from a privilege into a public right. During the nineteenth century, governments in Europe and North America began to build free schools for all children, believing that a literate population was essential for industry and democracy. Universities expanded, science entered the curriculum, and teaching became a trained profession. By the middle of the twentieth century, primary education was nearly universal in the developed world and was spreading quickly through Asia and Africa.

Today, education is being reshaped once again, this time by technology. A student in a small village can now attend lessons recorded on another continent, practise with interactive materials and sit standardised tests such as IELTS entirely online. Yet the central purpose has not changed since the earliest times: education exists to prepare each new generation to understand its world and to improve it.
